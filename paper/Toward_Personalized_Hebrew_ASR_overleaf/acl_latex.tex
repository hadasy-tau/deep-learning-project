% ABSTRACT

\begin{abstract}
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems are evaluated by error rates averaged across many speakers, which says little
about how well they serve any one person, yet many deployments serve a single speaker. We ask two questions: does a Hebrew ASR model
serve all its speakers equally, and how can it be adapted to a single speaker?
On 267 speakers in Knesset committee recordings, a Hebrew fine-tune of Whisper
lowers every speaker's error. The gain varies between speakers, and somewhat
between groups such as fast and slow speakers, but speakers largely keep their
order. We then adapt the Hebrew model to 12 speakers with LoRA. With 80 minutes
of a speaker's speech, the adapter lowers the word error rate by a median 12\%,
and an adapter trained on the same amount of other speakers' speech lowers it by
10\%, so the speaker's own speech adds 2--3\%. We also train four speakers on up
to 6 hours of their speech, and two of them on up to 24. Their adapters keep improving, up to a 34\% lower
word error rate than the Hebrew model, and a personal margin of up to 4\%
remains. Most of what adaptation buys is shared across speakers. Our code is
available at \url{https://github.com/hadasy-tau/deep-learning-project} and our
data at \url{https://huggingface.co/knesset-asr}.
\end{abstract}

% INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}

Modern ASR models such as Whisper \citep{radford2023whisper} are evaluated by
error rates averaged over many speakers, which can hide the speakers a
model serves poorly. Yet many deployments serve one person, such as a
Knesset member transcribed every day or the single user of a voice assistant, and
what matters to them is how well the model recognizes their own voice. This calls
for personalization: adapting a general model to one speaker with their own speech.

We study personalization for Hebrew and ask two questions: does a Hebrew ASR
model serve all its speakers equally, and how can it be adapted to a single
speaker? We answer them on Knesset committee recordings, which the Hebrew model
was not trained on and which hold thousands of hours of speech from hundreds of
identified speakers. Our contributions are threefold. First, a speaker-level
error map of a general Whisper model and its Hebrew fine-tune over 267 speakers,
the first for Hebrew. Second, adaptation of the Hebrew model to individual speakers
with LoRA, on 5 minutes up to 24 hours of a speaker's speech, with the training
settings tuned in 64 runs on validation data. The adapters lower the speakers'
error, and to tell how much of this gain comes from the speaker's own speech, we
compare each adapter with one trained on the same amount of other speakers'
speech. Third, we find that most of what adaptation buys is shared across
speakers: adapters keep improving with more data, and the speaker's own speech
adds a margin over other speakers' speech that does not grow
with more data. In practice, one adapter trained on speakers from the same
setting can serve many speakers, without training a separate model for each.
Training is cheap: an adapter on 80 minutes of speech
trains in a few minutes on a standard GPU.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Hebrew ASR.}
Whisper \citep{radford2023whisper} is much weaker on languages with little
training data, Hebrew among them. ivrit.ai narrowed this gap by fine-tuning it on
Hebrew, mostly Knesset plenary speech \citep{marmor2025hebrewasr}, the starting
point for our work.

\paragraph{Gaps within a language.}
Within a single language, too, ASR serves some people worse than others. Error
rates differ by race \citep{koenecke2020racial}, and by gender, age and accent \citep{feng2021quantifying}. These studies compare groups, and we know of no such
analysis for Hebrew.  Adapting to a single typical speaker, \citet{weninger2019listen} find gains
that keep growing up to 20 hours of the speaker's speech, measured against the
unadapted model.

\section{Experimental Setup}


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

Our study has two experiments. In the first, a general Whisper model and its
Hebrew fine-tune (Section~\ref{sec:models}) transcribe the same speech, and we
compare the error of each speaker and group under the two models. In the second, we select 12 speakers from this map: speakers
the fine-tune helped least, speakers hard under both models, typical speakers, a
speaker with a low word error rate (WER), and two speakers the fine-tune served well. We then adapt the Hebrew model to each speaker's own speech
with LoRA, on 5 to 80 minutes and, for four speakers, up to 6 hours, and for two of them up to 24. We compare each adapter with a control trained on the
same amount of speech from other speakers, which separates learning the voice
from learning committee speech in general.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use \texttt{ivrit-ai/knesset-committees}, recordings of Israeli parliamentary
committee sessions with their human-written protocols, force-aligned to the
audio by a Whisper-family model so that every protocol word has a time span. Each protocol segment carries an
alignment quality score, the median probability the
aligner gives its words (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). The protocols are stenographic rather than
verbatim, so a low score may reflect a mismatch between the text and the audio,
and the protocol text is the reference for every WER we report (see
Limitations). The
corpus spans November 2018 to January 2026 with 13{,}177 sessions and 15{,}466
recorded hours, of which 7{,}886 are aligned speech with quality of at least 0.7.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

We compare two models. Arm~\textbf{A} is
\texttt{openai/whisper-large-v3} \citep{radford2023whisper}, the general
multilingual model, and arm~\textbf{B} is \texttt{ivrit-ai/whisper-large-v3}
\citep{marmor2025hebrewasr}, the same model fine-tuned on roughly 4{,}700 hours
of Hebrew \emph{plenary} speech, so our committee recordings are new to it,
though many of the speakers are not (see Limitations). Every adapter in this
work is trained and evaluated on the full arm~B. For the speaker-level analysis, arm~B is served
through ivrit.ai's inference checkpoint (\texttt{whisper-large-v3-turbo-ct2},
with 4 decoder layers instead of 32).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The released corpus needs two steps before use: a speaker-level analysis needs every
protocol segment linked to a verified person, and Whisper takes at most 30 seconds of audio
while sessions last hours. We follow the procedure of VoxKnesset
\citep{marmor2026voxknesset}, a 2{,}300-hour corpus of Knesset plenary speech
with verified speakers, and apply it to the committee recordings. We do not
use VoxKnesset's audio, which comes from the plenary sessions
arm~B was fine-tuned on.


For the first, we match each protocol speaker name exactly to the Knesset's
official roster of name variants, within the member's term of service, and
discard names that do not match, since a wrong identity would be worse than a
missing one. The matched identity also gives each member's birth year, gender,
origin, nationality and religion, which we use to group speakers. For the second,
each identified speaker turn is cut into pieces of at most 30 seconds, which we
call chunks, that never cross a speaker change, with the aligned protocol text
as the reference. The result is 1{,}204{,}617 chunks (3{,}841 hours) from 330
current and former Knesset members in 10{,}905 sessions.

Chunks with alignment quality below 0.7 are excluded from all analyses,
and training and validation data are further restricted to chunks of at least 0.95,
following ivrit.ai's recommendation, that also pass a word-level check
(Appendix~\ref{app:data}).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Speaker-Level Performance}

Both models transcribe the same chunks: up to one hour of speech per speaker, spread across
many of their sessions, 65{,}990 chunks
(230 hours) from 267 speakers in all. Arm~A runs through Hugging Face Inference
Providers and arm~B through a RunPod endpoint, and both are told the language is Hebrew, so that neither is scored
on language detection. For each speaker and
group, we report WER under both models, pooled
over the speaker's chunks (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}), the gain
$\text{WER}_A-\text{WER}_B$, and the relative gain,
$(\text{WER}_A-\text{WER}_B)/\text{WER}_A$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Personalization}
\label{sec:personalization}

\paragraph{Speakers and data.}
The sessions of each of the 12 selected speakers are split into training,
validation and test sets that share no session, with the newest sessions held
out for testing (Appendix~\ref{app:additional}). Each speaker is trained on budgets of 5,
20 and 80 minutes of their own speech, nested and filled from their most recent
training sessions first.  Every model is scored on two test sets from the
same held-out sessions (Appendix~\ref{app:data}): chunks at quality $\geq 0.95$
(\emph{clean test}, about 45 minutes per speaker) and all chunks at quality
$\geq 0.7$ (\emph{full test}, a median of 90 minutes, depending on how much of the
speaker's speech aligns well). The clean test keeps chunks whose protocol
matches the audio, so its reference is closest to what was said, and the full
test checks that our results do not depend on a filter set by a Whisper-family
aligner (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). For each speaker, every model is scored on the
same chunks of a test set.

\paragraph{Adapter and training.}
For each
speaker and budget, we train a LoRA adapter \citep{hu2022lora} on arm~B: added
low-rank weights of rank 8 on the query and value projections of every attention
layer (3.9M trainable parameters), while the model's own weights stay frozen.  Training runs for at most 400 steps,
more beyond 80 minutes, with early stopping on the speaker's validation loss (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}).  At 80 minutes,
validation loss bottoms out after about two passes over the data, and every run
stops well before the step cap (Figure~\ref{fig:lcspeakers},
Appendix~\ref{app:additional}). 

\paragraph{Choosing the training settings.}
We tune on validation data only, in 64 runs, with four speakers, one of each
kind (helped least, hard under both models, typical and low WER), at 5 and 80
minutes, and 20 minutes uses the 80-minute choice. A run is scored by how much
it lowers validation loss relative to arm~B, $(L_B - L_\text{best})/L_B$, with $L_B$
arm~B's validation loss and $L_\text{best}$ the adapter's lowest. Tuning proceeds in three stages,
each starting from the previous choice: the learning rate ($10^{-4}$,
$3\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$), then the LoRA rank (8, 16) with Whisper's dropout
(0, 0.1), then augmentation (SpecAugment, alone or with $\pm10\%$ tempo changes,
but no pitch or speed perturbation, which would change the voice).
A change replaces the default (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, rank 8, no dropout,
no augmentation) only if it raises the score by at least 0.01 on average and for
three of the four speakers. Raising Whisper's dropout removes most of the
adapter's improvement, while neither rank 16 nor augmentation improves the score
by the required margin (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}, Appendix~\ref{app:training}).

\paragraph{Control.}
To separate what an adapter learns about the speaker from what it learns about
the committee setting, such as rooms, microphones and vocabulary, the 12 speakers are split at random into two folds of six. For
each fold and budget, a \emph{control} adapter is trained with the same settings
on the same number of minutes from the six speakers of the \emph{other} fold,
so it never heard the speakers it is scored on.
 We report the relative gain over
arm~B, $(\text{WER}_B-\text{WER}_\text{adapted})/\text{WER}_B$, and the
\textbf{personal margin},
$(\text{WER}_\text{control}-\text{WER}_\text{own})/\text{WER}_B$, which equals
the own gain minus the control gain. Every comparison between two models, including the
personal margin, gets a 95\% interval and a p-value from a paired bootstrap over
test chunks (Appendix~\ref{app:significance}), and we call a difference
significant when $p<0.05$. All
budgets use seed 0, and at 80 minutes seeds 1 and 2 repeat the own adapters and
the controls, with the folds redrawn, to separate speaker-level effects from
seed variance.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Speaker-Level Error Map}
\label{sec:errormap}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/error_map_hist.png}
    \caption{Per-speaker WER under arm~A (general) and arm~B (Hebrew fine-tune),
    all 267 speakers. Arm~B shifts the whole distribution down, and its spread shrinks only in proportion to the lower average.}
    \label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

After the quality filter, the analysis covers 267 speakers and 217 hours
(58{,}180 chunks), with an overall WER, pooled over all chunks, of 0.387 for arm~A and 0.292 for arm~B.
Statistics below are over the 261 speakers with at least 20 chunks, and
per-speaker 95\% confidence intervals come from bootstrapping each speaker's chunks
(Appendix~\ref{app:boot-errormap}).

\paragraph{The fine-tune helps every speaker, and speakers keep their order.}
All 261 speakers have a lower WER under arm~B, by a median 24\% of arm~A's error
(11\% to 58\%), and every improvement is beyond sampling noise (Figure~\ref{fig:gain}, Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).
Yet the spread of per-speaker WER shrinks (standard deviation 0.099 to 0.069)
only in proportion to the lower average (coefficient of variation 0.25 and 0.23,
Figure~\ref{fig:hist}). Speakers keep their order (Spearman $\rho=0.90$ between
WER$_A$ and WER$_B$), and this ranking is stable across a speaker's recordings
(Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).

\paragraph{Group membership explains little.}
We compare the gain across seven groupings of speakers: gender, nationality,
religion, religious orientation, country of origin, age quartile and
speaking-rate tertile. The clearest difference is speaking rate
(Figure~\ref{fig:rate}, with the other six in Figure~\ref{fig:groups}): slow
speakers gain more than fast ones (27\% vs.\ 22\% of arm~A's error removed).
Arab, Muslim, Druze and Bedouin speakers also gain more than Jewish speakers
(29--30\% vs.\ 24\%). But group sizes are highly imbalanced (229 Jewish speakers
against 5 Bedouin), and the variation within each group far exceeds the
differences between group medians. A speaker's group thus says little about how
much the fine-tune helps them, which motivates adapting to individual speakers.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Adaptation Results}
\label{sec:adaptation}

\paragraph{The 80-minute adapter improves almost every speaker.}
With 80 minutes and seed 0, the own adapter lowers arm~B's WER by a median
12.0\% on the clean test and 15.3\% on the full test (Figure~\ref{fig:budget},
blue). It is better than arm~B for 11 of 12 speakers on the clean test (9
significantly) and for all 12 on the full test (all significantly). The other
two seeds agree: over all 36 speaker--seed pairs, it beats arm~B in 35 on the
clean test and in all 36 on the full test, and averaged over the three seeds,
the clean-test gains range from +3\% to +28\% (median +11.4\%,
Figure~\ref{fig:bestmodel}). Figure~\ref{fig:wermedian} (Appendix~\ref{app:additional}) shows the absolute WERs behind these gains.

    12 speakers). Each dot is one speaker, and bars and numbers are medians. The median
    personal margin is the median of per-speaker differences.}
    \label{fig:budget}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Personal versus shared adaptation.}
The own adapter's median relative gain over arm~B grows from +4.3\% to +7.5\% to
+12.0\% on the clean test at 5, 20 and 80 minutes, and from +4.2\% to +9.7\% to
+15.3\% on the full test (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}). The control, which never heard
the speaker, rises almost as fast, to +0.3\%, +7.8\% and +10.1\% on the clean test
and +6.7\%, +10.9\% and +14.3\% on the full test. Most of the adaptation gain is
therefore shared across speakers, and the speaker's own audio adds a median personal
margin of +2.6\% and +2.3\% at 80 minutes, which stays between +1.6\% and +3.1\% on
the clean test at every budget. No 5- or 20-minute adapter is the best model for any
speaker (Figure~\ref{fig:bestmodel}), and at 5 minutes both adapters are unreliable:
they are worse than arm~B for some speakers, and the personal margin has opposite
signs on the two test sets.


The margin differs between speakers, and it is not larger for the speakers the
fine-tune helped least: it is largest for 23558 (David Bitan), at +9\% to +10\% on the full test
in every seed, and absent for two of those three
(Figure~\ref{fig:perspeaker}, Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).


\paragraph{Beyond 80 minutes.}

To test whether the personal margin is limited by the amount of speaker data,
we train four speakers on up to 360 minutes (6 hours), and
two of them on up to 1{,}440 minutes (24 hours): 23558 (David Bitan), whose margin is the largest,
and 23641 (Yaakov Asher), a speaker outside the 12 with one of the highest WERs. Each budget has its own control
(Figures~\ref{fig:curve} and~\ref{fig:curve6h}, Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).
The own adapters keep improving with diminishing returns, up to a 34\% lower WER
than arm~B. The control improves as fast, so the added gain is shared across
speakers, and the personal margin does not grow. It shrinks as a share of the
gain but does not vanish, and at 24 hours it is 2--4\%. Raising the LoRA
rank from 8 to 32 does not change this conclusion (Appendix~\ref{app:training}).

\section{Conclusion}

Personalizing Hebrew ASR works, and it needs less than it seems. A general
Hebrew fine-tune leaves the gaps between speakers in place, but a small LoRA
adapter cuts a speaker's errors substantially, by up to 34\%. Most of
that gain does not require the speaker's own voice: speech from other speakers
in the same setting delivers it, and the speaker's own recordings add a smaller
margin on top. A practical system can therefore start from one adapter per
setting and refine it per speaker, and keep collecting speech from
that setting, since more data keeps improving a speaker's model even when the
personal margin does not grow. Future work could adapt on similar rather than
random speakers, and design training objectives that separate the voice from the
setting.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Prior exposure.} Arm~B was fine-tuned on Knesset plenary speech, which includes many of our
speakers. Our evaluation uses committee recordings, a different setting and
register, but the model may still be partly familiar with these voices.

\paragraph{Shared recording conditions.} 
Train and test sets are session-disjoint but not
committee-disjoint: 75--82\% of the test speech comes from committees the speaker
also appears in during training. Part of the personal margin may therefore
reflect familiar rooms and microphones rather than the voice.

\paragraph{The reference is the protocol.} Every WER is measured against the stenographic protocol, which is not a verbatim
transcript: stenographers condense, rephrase and omit speech, so part of every
measured error is a mismatch between protocol and audio rather than a
recognition mistake. Our gains are gains in agreement with the protocol, and may
partly reflect learning what the stenographer omits. We limit this by training
and validating only on chunks whose protocol closely matches the audio
(quality $\geq 0.95$,
Appendix~\ref{app:data}) and by scoring every model on two test sets
(Section~\ref{sec:personalization}). No verbatim
reference was available, so our comparisons are most reliable between models
scored against the same references.

\paragraph{Limited compute.} All experiments ran on paid cloud services (RunPod GPUs and Hugging Face
inference), which limited the models, configurations
and speakers we could explore.

\section{Data Preparation Details}
\label{app:data}

\paragraph{Alignment quality score.}
The corpus aligns each protocol to its recording with a Whisper-family forced
aligner, which assigns every protocol word a time span and a probability: how
likely the model finds that word at that point in the audio. A protocol
segment's quality score is the median probability of its words. Our chunks of
up to 30 seconds can join several protocol segments, so their score is the
duration-weighted median over those parts. A low score means the text and the
audio disagree, potentially because the stenographer condensed or omitted
speech, or because of cross-talk. ivrit.ai treat 0.65 as fit for training and
0.95 as high quality.

\paragraph{Quality filter for the analysis.}
The 0.7 threshold removes 7{,}810 of 65{,}990 chunks (11.8\%, 12.8 hours).
Removing them lowers the overall WER from 0.417 to 0.387 (A) and from 0.324 to
0.292 (B), and leaves the difference between the arms almost unchanged.

\paragraph{Quality filter for training.}
Because the score is a median, a chunk can pass it while a few of its words
are misaligned. Training and validation chunks therefore use the word
probabilities directly and must pass four conditions: quality at least 0.95,
at most 10\% of words with probability below 0.5, no run of three or more such
words, and the aligned words inside the chunk's time span must account for its
reference text to within 20\%, so that the checked probabilities cover the whole
text.

\paragraph{Two test sets.}
The aligner is itself a Whisper-family model, so a 0.95 threshold may keep the
chunks that Whisper models already transcribe well. Per chunk, the score
predicts arm~A's WER about as well as arm~B's (Spearman $\rho=-0.62$ and $-0.63$
over the 65{,}990 chunks), so it does not favour the Hebrew fine-tune over the
general model, and filtering on it does not tilt the comparison between them.
It does mean that the filtered test set leans toward speech that every model we compare finds
easy. We therefore score every model on two test sets drawn from the same
held-out sessions: the filtered chunks, and all chunks with quality of at
least 0.7.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Implementation Details}
\label{app:implementation}

\paragraph{Training.}
Adapters are trained with PyTorch, Hugging Face \texttt{transformers}
(\texttt{Seq2SeqTrainer}) and \texttt{peft}, on the frozen base model in
bfloat16. Each control's pool takes
one chunk from each of its six speakers in turn until the budget is reached,
and never from a session in which a speaker it is scored on is tested or
validated.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
Reference and hypothesis are normalized (niqqud and punctuation removed) and
aligned word by word, and substitutions, deletions and insertions are counted
per chunk. WER is pooled over a speaker's test set: the sum of word errors
divided by the sum of reference words. Confidence intervals and p-values come from
a paired bootstrap over test chunks (Appendix~\ref{app:significance}).

\paragraph{Adapter capacity.}
To check whether a rank-8 adapter is too small to hold the voice, we repeat the
1{,}440-minute own adapters of 23558 and 23641 and their control at rank 32. The
larger adapter does not make the personal margin grow: it rises in some cases and
falls in others (Table~\ref{tab:capacity}).

\paragraph{Reliability of the per-speaker measures.}
Each speaker is measured on about one hour of speech, so their WER and gain
carry sampling noise. To test whether the differences between speakers are a
stable property of the speakers rather than of the particular sample, we split
each speaker's sessions into two alternating halves, compute WER$_B$ and the
relative gain on each half independently, and compare the halves across speakers
(Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}). If the differences were noise, the two halves
would be unrelated, and if they are stable, the points follow the diagonal. They
do: the correlation between halves, corrected to full length (Spearman--Brown),
gives a reliability of 0.78 for WER$_B$ and 0.75 for relative gain. This is what
justifies ranking speakers by their gain when selecting them for
personalization.

\section{Bootstrap Intervals and Significance}
\label{app:boot-errormap}\label{app:significance}


Every interval and p-value in this paper comes from bootstrapping a speaker's
chunks. A resampled set draws the speaker's chunks at random with replacement
until it is the size of the original, every model in a comparison is scored on the
same resampled set, and each set gives pooled WERs (the sum of word errors divided
by the sum of reference words) and their difference. The 95\% interval is the 2.5th
to 97.5th percentile of the resampled differences.


\paragraph{Error map.}
We draw 1{,}000 sets per speaker, for WER$_A$, WER$_B$ and the gain
$\text{WER}_A-\text{WER}_B$. No p-value is computed: a speaker's improvement under
arm~B is beyond sampling noise when the interval of the gain lies entirely above
zero, as it does for all 261 speakers with at least 20 chunks.

\paragraph{Adaptation.}
We draw 2{,}000 sets of each speaker's test chunks, for the gain of an adapter
over arm~B, $\text{WER}_B-\text{WER}_\text{adapted}$, and for the personal margin,
$\text{WER}_\text{control}-\text{WER}_\text{own}$. Dividing by WER$_B$ gives the
relative units of Section~\ref{sec:adaptation}. The p-value is two-sided, since an
own adapter could also do worse than its control: it is twice the share of the
differences that fall on the far side of zero.
