% ABSTRACT

\begin{abstract}
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems are built for general-purpose use
and evaluated by error rates averaged across many speakers, which says little
about how well they serve any one person. Yet in many deployments it is a single
speaker's accuracy that matters. We ask how to personalize a Hebrew ASR model to
one speaker under a small data and compute budget.
On Knesset committee recordings, we first measure, for each of 267
speakers, the error of a general Whisper model and of its Hebrew fine-tune.
The fine-tune lowers every speaker's error but leaves speakers as unequal as
before, and group membership explains little of who is served well. We then
adapt the Hebrew model to 12 speakers with LoRA, and compare each speaker's own
adapter with a control adapter trained on the same amount of speech from other
speakers. With 80 minutes of a speaker's speech, the own adapter lowers the
word error rate by a median 12\%, but the control already lowers it by 10\%, so
the personal gain is only 2--3\%. With up to 24 hours of a speaker's speech,
the own adapter keeps improving, but the control improves as fast, and the
personal gain does not grow. Most of what adaptation buys is shared across
speakers, so a claim of personalization needs a control of this kind. Our code is available at
\url{https://github.com/hadasy-tau/deep-learning-project}.
\end{abstract}

% INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}

Modern ASR models such as Whisper \citep{radford2023whisper} are evaluated by
error rates averaged over many speakers, an average that can hide the speakers a
model serves poorly. Yet many deployments serve one particular person, such as a
Knesset member transcribed every day or the single user of a voice assistant.
What matters to them is how well the model recognizes their own voice, which
calls for personalization: adapting a general model to one speaker with a small
amount of their own speech.

We study personalization for Hebrew and ask three questions: does a Hebrew
fine-tune serve all speakers equally, what does a speaker's own speech add over
the same amount of other speakers' speech, and does that personal gain grow with
more of the speaker's data? We answer them on Knesset committee recordings,
which the Hebrew model was not trained on, and compare each speaker's own LoRA
adapter with a control trained on the same amount of other speakers'
speech. Our contributions
are threefold. First, a speaker-level error map, the first for Hebrew ASR, shows
that the Hebrew fine-tune helps every speaker but leaves them as unequal as
before. Second, a controlled comparison shows that at 80 minutes a speaker's own
audio adds only 2--3\% over the control. Third, a data curve to 24 hours of one
speaker's speech shows that this personal margin does not grow. Before scoring
on the test sets, we tuned the learning rate, LoRA rank, Whisper's dropout and
augmentation in 64 runs on validation data, and at 24 hours we also tried a four
times larger adapter. Training is cheap: an adapter on 80 minutes of speech
trains in a few minutes on a standard GPU.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Averages hide who is served.}
Whisper \citep{radford2023whisper} is much weaker on languages with little
training data, Hebrew among them. ivrit.ai closed this gap by fine-tuning it on
Hebrew, mostly Knesset plenary speech \citep{marmor2025hebrewasr}, the starting
point for our work. Both models are evaluated by error rates averaged over large
test sets, never per speaker or subgroup.

\paragraph{Gaps within a language.}
Within a single language, too, ASR serves some people worse than others. Error
rates differ by race \citep{koenecke2020racial}, and \citet{tatman2017gender},
\citet{feng2021quantifying} and \citet{liu2022fairness} find gaps by gender,
dialect, age and accent. These studies compare groups, and we know of no such
analysis for Hebrew. We provide one, for each speaker and for subgroups that
share gender, age, origin or speaking rate.

\paragraph{Adapting a model to one speaker.}
Adapting a recognizer to its user is an old idea in speech recognition
\citep{gales1998mllr}. With modern models, fine-tuning on a small amount of a
person's own speech can greatly reduce their errors, but this has mostly been
studied for atypical speech, such as speech impairments \citep{green2021disordered}
and strong accents \citep{shor2019personalizing}. Training a full model for
every speaker is expensive, so methods such as LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} train only
a small number of added parameters, and have also been used with Whisper
\citep{liu2024s2lora}. For typical speakers, \citet{weninger2019listen}
report gains that keep growing up to 20 hours of a speaker's speech, but measure
them against the unadapted model only, so they also include what any in-domain
speech would buy. We separate the two with a control trained on the same amount
of other speakers' speech.

\section{Experimental Setup}


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Overview}

Our study has two experiments. In the first, a general Whisper model and its
Hebrew fine-tune (Section~\ref{sec:models}) transcribe the same speech, and we
measure the error of each speaker and subgroup under both, and so the gain the
fine-tune brings. In the second, we select 12 speakers from this map: speakers
the fine-tune helped least, speakers hard under both models, typical speakers, a
low-WER speaker, and two speakers the fine-tune served well. To avoid selecting
on measurement noise, we rank speakers on their earlier sessions only and hold
out their newest sessions for testing. We then fine-tune the Hebrew model on
each speaker's own speech and compare it with a control adapter trained on the
same amount of speech from other speakers, which separates learning the voice
from learning committee speech in general.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use \texttt{ivrit-ai/knesset-committees}, recordings of Israeli parliamentary
committee sessions with their human-written protocols, force-aligned to the
audio. Each protocol segment carries the speaker's name and an
alignment-confidence score. Because the protocols are stenographic rather than
verbatim, a low score may reflect a mismatch between the text and the audio. The
corpus spans November 2018 to January 2026 with 13{,}177 sessions and 15{,}466
recorded hours, of which 7{,}886 are aligned speech with confidence above 0.7.


% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

We compare two models with the same architecture. Arm~\textbf{A} is
\texttt{openai/whisper-large-v3} \citep{radford2023whisper}, the general
multilingual model, and arm~\textbf{B} is \texttt{ivrit-ai/whisper-large-v3}
\citep{marmor2025hebrewasr}, the same model fine-tuned on roughly 4{,}700 hours
of Hebrew \emph{plenary} speech, so our committee recordings are new to it,
though many of the speakers are not (see Limitations). Every adapter in this
work is trained on top of arm~B. For the speaker-level analysis, arm~B is served
through ivrit.ai's inference checkpoint (\texttt{whisper-large-v3-turbo-ct2},
with 4 decoder layers instead of 32).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The released corpus needs two steps before use: Whisper takes at most 30 seconds
of audio while sessions last hours, and a speaker-level analysis needs every
segment linked to a verified person. We follow the procedure of VoxKnesset
\citep{marmor2026voxknesset}, a 2{,}300-hour corpus of Knesset plenary speech
with verified speakers, but not its audio, which comes from the plenary sessions
arm~B was fine-tuned on.

Protocol speaker names are matched exactly to the Knesset's official roster of
name variants and accepted only within the member's term of service, and
unresolved names are discarded. Each member's birth year, gender and origin are
joined through the matched identity. Each identified turn is then cut into
segments of at most 30 seconds that never cross a speaker change, with the
aligned protocol text as the reference. The result is 1{,}204{,}617 segments
(3{,}841 hours) of 330 current and former Knesset members in 10{,}905 sessions.

Each segment carries an alignment quality score between 0 and 1
(Appendix~\ref{app:data}). Segments below 0.7 are excluded from all analyses,
and training and validation data are further restricted to segments above 0.95,
following ivrit.ai's recommendation.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Speaker-Level Performance}

Both models transcribe the same segments: one hour of speech per speaker, drawn
round-robin over their sessions so that it spans their tenure, 65{,}990 segments
(230 hours) from 267 speakers in all. Arm~A runs through Hugging Face Inference
Providers and arm~B through a RunPod endpoint with ivrit.ai's inference
checkpoint, and both are told the language is Hebrew. For each speaker and
subgroup, we report WER and CER under both models, the gain
$\text{WER}_A-\text{WER}_B$, and the relative gain,
$(\text{WER}_A-\text{WER}_B)/\text{WER}_A$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Personalization}
\label{sec:personalization}

\paragraph{Speakers and data.}
The sessions of each of the 12 selected speakers are split into training,
validation and test sets that share no session, with the newest sessions held
out for testing (Appendix~\ref{app:additional}). Each speaker is trained on 5,
20 and 80 minutes of their own speech, nested and filled from their most recent
training sessions first, and four speakers later on up to 1{,}440 minutes
(Appendix~\ref{app:training}). Every model is scored on two test sets from the
same held-out sessions (Appendix~\ref{app:data}): the high-quality segments
(\emph{clean test}, median 47 minutes per speaker) and all segments at quality
$\geq 0.7$ (\emph{full test}, median 90 minutes).

\paragraph{Adapter and training.}
We train LoRA adapters \citep{hu2022lora} of rank 8 on the query and value
projections of every attention layer of arm~B (3.9M trainable parameters), with
the learning rate and the other settings chosen on validation data
(Appendix~\ref{app:training}), for at most 400 steps with early stopping:
validation loss is measured every 20 steps on other sessions of the speaker,
used for neither training nor testing, and training stops after four checks
without improvement, restoring the best checkpoint. The training labels are the
protocol text.

\paragraph{Control.}
An adapter trained on a speaker's audio learns both the speaker and the
committee setting: rooms, microphones, vocabulary and the protocol's style. To
separate the two, the 12 speakers are split at random into two folds of six. For
each fold and budget, a \emph{control} adapter is trained with the same recipe
on the same number of minutes from the six speakers of the \emph{other} fold.
Each speaker is scored on the same test segments under their own adapter and
under the control, which never heard them. We report the relative gain over
arm~B, $(\text{WER}_B-\text{WER}_\text{adapted})/\text{WER}_B$, and the
\textbf{personalization},
$(\text{WER}_\text{control}-\text{WER}_\text{own})/\text{WER}_B$, which equals
the own gain minus the control gain. Its 95\% interval and p-value come from a
paired bootstrap over test segments (Appendix~\ref{app:significance}). All
budgets use seed 0, and at 80 minutes seeds 1 and 2 repeat the own adapters and
the controls, with the folds redrawn, to separate speaker-level effects from
seed variance.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Speaker-Level Error Map}
\label{sec:errormap}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/error_map_hist.png}
    \caption{Per-speaker WER under arm~A (general) and arm~B (Hebrew fine-tune),
    all 267 speakers. Arm~B shifts the whole distribution down without narrowing it
    in proportion.}
    \label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

After the quality filter, the analysis covers 267 speakers and 217 hours
(58{,}180 segments), with a corpus WER of 0.387 for arm~A and 0.292 for arm~B.
Statistics below are over the 261 speakers with at least 20 segments, and
per-speaker 95\% confidence intervals come from bootstrapping each speaker's segments
(Appendix~\ref{app:boot-errormap}).

\paragraph{The fine-tune helps every speaker but leaves them as unequal.}
All 261 speakers have a lower WER under arm~B, and for every one of them the
improvement is beyond sampling noise: the 95\% bootstrap interval of their gain
lies entirely above zero (Figure~\ref{fig:gain}, Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).
Yet the spread of per-speaker WER shrinks (standard deviation 0.099 to 0.069)
only in proportion to the lower average (coefficient of variation 0.25 and 0.23,
Figure~\ref{fig:hist}). Speakers keep their order (Spearman $\rho=0.90$ between
WER$_A$ and WER$_B$), and this ranking is stable across a speaker's recordings
(Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).

\paragraph{Group membership explains little.}
We compare the gain across seven groupings of speakers: gender, nationality,
religion, religious orientation, country of origin, age quartile and
speaking-rate tertile. The clearest difference is speaking rate
(Figure~\ref{fig:rate}, with the other six in Figure~\ref{fig:groups}): slow
speakers gain more than fast ones (27\% vs.\ 22\% of arm~A's error removed).
Arab, Muslim, Druze and Bedouin speakers also gain more than Jewish speakers
(29--30\% vs.\ 24\%). But group sizes are highly imbalanced (229 Jewish speakers
against 5 Bedouin), and the variation within each group far exceeds the
differences between group medians. A speaker's group thus says little about how
well the model serves them, which motivates adapting to individual speakers.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Adaptation Results}
\label{sec:adaptation}

\paragraph{The 80-minute adapter improves almost every speaker.}
With 80 minutes and seed 0, the own adapter lowers arm~B's WER by a median
12.0\% on the clean test and 15.3\% on the full test (Figure~\ref{fig:budget},
blue). It is better than arm~B for 11 of 12 speakers on the clean test (9
significantly) and for all 12 on the full test (all significantly). The other
two seeds agree: over all 36 speaker--seed pairs, it beats arm~B in 35 on the
clean test and in all 36 on the full test, and averaged over the three seeds,
the clean-test gains range from +3\% to +28\% (median +11.4\%,
Figure~\ref{fig:bestmodel}).

\begin{figure}[t]

    other speakers, and green the personalization (own $-$ control). Each dot is one

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Personal versus shared adaptation.}
At 80 minutes, the median personalization over the 36 speaker--seed pairs is
+2.4\% on the clean test and +2.2\% on the full test. With seed 0, the control,
which never heard the speaker, already gains +10.1\% and +14.3\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:budget}), against the own adapter's +12.0\% and +15.3\%: most
of the adaptation gain is general, and the speaker's own audio adds 2--3\% on
top of it.

Five speakers are significant in all three seeds on the full test
(Figure~\ref{fig:perspeaker}), led by 23558 at +9\% to +10\%, while six never
are, in any seed or test. Profile does not predict the effect: speakers the
fine-tune helped least and speakers hard under both models fall on both sides. A
speaker's personalization varies across seeds with a median standard deviation
of 2.4\% on the clean test, as large as the effect, so we make per-speaker
claims only where all seeds agree. Nor does the margin grow on cleaner audio: by
alignment-quality band of the full test, own adapter and control gain alike on
the two hardest bands (about +20\%), and the median personalization is +0.5\%,
+4.0\%, +2.5\% and +2.3\% from the hardest band to the cleanest.

\paragraph{How much speaker data is needed.}
The own adapter's median relative gain over arm~B grows from +4.3\% to +7.5\% to
+12.0\% on the clean test at 5, 20 and 80 minutes, and from +4.2\% to +9.7\% to
+15.3\% on the full test (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}), and no 5- or 20-minute
adapter is the best model for any speaker (Figure~\ref{fig:bestmodel}). The
control rises almost as fast, to +0.3\%, +7.8\% and +10.1\% on the clean test
and +6.7\%, +10.9\% and +14.3\% on the full test, so what extra audio buys is
mostly not specific to the speaker, and the personal margin stays between +1.6\%
and +3.1\% on the clean test at every budget. At 5 minutes both adapters are
unreliable: they are worse than arm~B for some speakers, and the personal margin
has opposite signs on the two test sets.

\paragraph{Beyond 80 minutes.}
To test whether personalization is limited by the amount of speaker data, we
train 23558, the speaker with the largest personal effect, and 23641, a speaker
outside the 12 with one of the highest WERs, on up to 1{,}440 minutes (24 hours)
of their own speech, each budget with its own control (Figure~\ref{fig:curve},
Appendix~\ref{app:training}). The own adapter keeps improving, with diminishing
returns: on the full test, from +29.4\% at 80 minutes to +34.3\% at 24 hours for
23558, and from +17.4\% to +23.4\% for 23641. The control improves at least as
fast (for 23558, from +19.7\% to +30.2\%), so the added gain comes mostly from
committee speech in general, and personalization does not grow. It shrinks as a
share of the gain, for 23558 from a third to an eighth, but it does not vanish:
at 24 hours it is +4.1\% for 23558 and +2.3\% for 23641. Longer training does
not change this: validation loss stops improving after about one pass over the
data, and a larger adapter (rank 32) does not help
(Appendix~\ref{app:training}). Two more speakers, trained on up to 360 minutes,
show the same pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:curve6h}).

\paragraph{Learning curves.}
Validation loss reaches its minimum at step 60--100, about two passes over the
data and 38--54\% below the validation loss of arm~B without an adapter, and
then rises as the adapter overfits (Figure~\ref{fig:lc}). Every run stops early,
by step 180 of 400, and the three seeds trace nearly identical curves
(Figure~\ref{fig:lcspeakers}, Appendix~\ref{app:additional}). An adapter trains
in a few minutes on a standard GPU.

\section{Conclusion}

The Hebrew fine-tune lowers every speaker's error but leaves them as unequal as
before, which motivates adapting to individual speakers.

\paragraph{Most of the adaptation gain is general.}
An 80-minute LoRA adapter on a speaker's own committee speech lowers arm~B's WER by a
median 12\% on the clean test and 15\% on the full test, and the same amount of other
speakers' audio already lowers it by 10\% and 14\%. Training on the speaker's own
audio adds a further 2--3\% over this shared baseline, positive in all three seeds.

\paragraph{More audio gives more gain, but mostly not personal gain.}
From 5 to 80 minutes the own adapter's gain roughly triples, and the control's grows
almost as fast. Up to 24 hours of a speaker's own speech, the own adapter keeps
improving, and a control trained on as much audio from other speakers improves as
fast: the added gain does not require the speaker's own speech, and the personal
margin does not grow with it.

\paragraph{Adaptation is cheap.}
An adapter converges in about two passes over its data and trains in a few
minutes on a standard GPU. The recipe is insensitive to
rank, augmentation and, at 80 minutes, the learning rate, but Whisper's dropout
prevents learning.

Since most of what adaptation buys is shared across speakers, a claim of
personalization needs a control trained on other speakers' speech. Further directions are adaptation on similar rather than random speakers, and training
objectives that separate the voice from the committee setting.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Prior exposure.} Arm~B was fine-tuned on Knesset plenary speech, which
includes many of our speakers. Our evaluation uses committee recordings, a different setting and register, but the model may still be partly familiar with these voices.

\paragraph{Shared recording conditions.} Train and test sets are meeting-disjoint,
but not committee-disjoint: about 75--82\% of the test speech comes from committees the same speaker also appears in during training, and so likely from the same rooms and microphones. Part of an adapter's gain may therefore reflect familiar recording conditions rather than the voice alone.

\paragraph{The reference is the protocol.} Every WER is measured against the
stenographic protocol, which is not a verbatim transcript. Our gains are gains in
agreement with the protocol, and may partly reflect learning what the stenographer
omits. We limit this by training and validating only on segments whose protocol
closely matches the audio (quality $\geq 0.95$, Appendix~\ref{app:data}). That score,
however, comes from a Whisper-family aligner, so a test set filtered the same way would
favour speech that Whisper models already transcribe well; we therefore score every
model both on the high-quality segments and on all segments at quality $\geq 0.7$.

% TODO (decide later): "The reference is the protocol" (Hadas) and "Reference
% transcripts" (Dolev) cover the same limitation. Both are kept for now. Keep one,
% or fold the ivrit.ai consultation point into the other.
\paragraph{Reference transcripts.} Our references are the Knesset's stenographic
protocols, not verbatim transcripts: stenographers condense, rephrase and omit
speech, so part of every measured error is a mismatch between protocol and audio
rather than a recognition mistake. We limit this with alignment-quality filters set
in consultation with the ivrit.ai team (Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing},
Appendix~\ref{app:data}), but no verbatim reference was available.
Absolute WERs therefore partly reflect the protocol's editing, and our comparisons
are most reliable between models scored against the same references.

\paragraph{Limited compute.} Training and inference ran on paid cloud services
(RunPod GPUs and Hugging Face inference), at a total cost of about \$200 across all
our experiments. Within this budget we focused on the experiments central to our
question, and a larger budget would allow more models, configurations and speakers
to be explored.

\paragraph{Tuning on validation loss.} The rank, dropout, augmentation and learning
rate were chosen on the validation loss of four speakers. The rejected settings,
including rank 16, were never scored on the test sets.

\paragraph{Few speakers beyond 80 minutes.} The longer budgets cover four speakers,
two of them up to 24 hours, and the rank-32 check uses one seed, so the finding that
the personal margin does not grow with more speaker data holds for these speakers,
and may not hold for every speaker.

\paragraph{A random control group.} The control pools speakers at random, so it does
not test whether \emph{similar} speakers could replace a speaker's own audio; we leave
that comparison to future work.

\section{Data Preparation Details}
\label{app:data}

\paragraph{Alignment quality score.}
The corpus aligns each protocol to its recording with a Whisper-family forced
aligner, which assigns every protocol word a time span and a probability: how
likely the model finds that word at that point in the audio. A protocol
segment's quality score is the median probability of its words. Our segments of
up to 30 seconds can join several protocol segments, so their score is the
duration-weighted median over those parts. A low score means the text and the
audio disagree, potentially because the stenographer condensed or omitted
speech, or because of cross-talk. ivrit.ai treat 0.65 as fit for training and
0.95 as high quality.

\paragraph{Quality filter for the analysis.}
The 0.7 threshold removes 7{,}810 of 65{,}990 segments (11.8\%, 12.8 hours).
Removing them lowers the corpus WER from 0.417 to 0.387 (A) and from 0.324 to
0.292 (B), and leaves the difference between the arms almost unchanged.

\paragraph{Quality filter for training.}
Because the score is a median, a segment can pass it while a few of its words
are misaligned. Training and validation segments therefore use the word
probabilities directly and must pass four conditions: confidence at least 0.95,
at most 10\% of words with probability below 0.5, no run of three or more such
words, and the aligned words inside the segment's time span must account for its
reference text to within 20\%, so that the checked probabilities cover the whole
text.

\paragraph{Two test sets.}
The aligner is itself a Whisper-family model, so a 0.95 threshold favours
segments that Whisper models already transcribe well. The score is not specific
to arm~B, though: per segment, it predicts arm~A's WER about as well as arm~B's
(Spearman $\rho=-0.62$ and $-0.63$ over the 65{,}990 segments). It mostly
measures what is hard for any model. Applied to the test set, the threshold
introduces a selection bias toward easy speech, so we score every model on two
test sets drawn from the same held-out sessions: the filtered segments, and all
segments with confidence of at least 0.7.

\section{Training Details}
\label{app:training}

We ran 64 tuning runs and evaluated each one on validation loss alone, and none
of them touched the test sets. Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} shows the chosen
settings, which are shared by every own and control adapter.

\paragraph{Tuning procedure.}
We chose four speakers for the tuning process, one per profile (30685 from S1,
23558 from S2, 30718 from S3 and 30859 from S4). They were trained at the two
ends of the budget range, 5 and 80 minutes, and 20 minutes takes the 80-minute
choice. Each run is scored by how much it lowers validation loss relative to
arm~B without an adapter: $(L_B - L_\text{best})/L_B$, where $L_B$ is arm~B's
validation loss and $L_\text{best}$ is the adapter's lowest validation loss
during training (the checkpoint early stopping restores). Tuning ran in three
stages, each starting from the previous stage's choice: the learning rate
($10^{-4}$, $3\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$), then LoRA rank $\{8, 16\}$ $\times$
Whisper's dropout $\{0, 0.1\}$, then augmentation (SpecAugment, alone or with
$\pm10\%$ tempo changes, but no pitch or speed perturbation, which would change
the voice). We started from a simple default setting (learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$, rank 8, no dropout, no augmentation) and kept it unless a
change helped clearly: a new setting replaced it only if it raised the relative
drop, averaged over the four speakers, by at least 0.01, and raised it for at
least three of the four.

\paragraph{Tuning results} (Figures~\ref{fig:tuningscores} and~\ref{fig:tuningcurves}).
Only the learning rate at 5 minutes and Whisper's dropout matter. At both
budgets a higher rate reaches its minimum validation loss earlier, but at 5
minutes the higher rates reach a worse minimum, so we use the lowest rate,
$10^{-4}$, while at 80 minutes all three reach nearly the same minimum and we
keep the default, $3\times10^{-4}$. Rank 16 matches rank 8 at 80 minutes and is
slightly worse at 5. Augmentation improves the relative drop by less than the
0.01 threshold at both budgets. Raising Whisper's own dropout to 0.1 removes
most of the adapter's improvement for every speaker.

\paragraph{Beyond 80 minutes.}
Four speakers are trained beyond 80 minutes: 23558 and 23641 up to 1{,}440 minutes,
and 30752 and 23635 up to 360. 23558 and 30752 are from the 12. 23641 and 23635 add
profiles the 12 lack: a Haredi speaker with one of the highest WERs, whom the Hebrew

full pass. At 1{,}440 minutes the best checkpoint comes after 0.83--1.11 passes, and
training stops at 1.02--1.23 passes: validation loss stops improving after about one
pass.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{app:implementation}

\paragraph{Training.}
Adapters are trained with PyTorch, Hugging Face \texttt{transformers}
(\texttt{Seq2SeqTrainer}) and \texttt{peft}, on the frozen base model in
bfloat16. Training segments are fed latest session first, so the 5-, 20- and
80-minute budgets are nested prefixes of one ordering. Each control's pool takes
one segment from each of its six speakers in turn until the budget is reached,
and never from a session in which a speaker it is scored on is tested or
validated.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
Reference and hypothesis are normalized (niqqud and punctuation removed) and
aligned word by word, and substitutions, deletions and insertions are counted
per segment. WER is pooled over a speaker's test set: the sum of word errors
divided by the sum of reference words. Decoding is greedy, and every
transcription passes a loop guard: a hypothesis whose zlib compression ratio
exceeds 3.0 is decoded again with no 6-token sequence repeated, and replaced if
the new one compresses less, identically for the base model, own adapters and
controls. Confidence intervals and p-values come from a paired bootstrap over
test segments (Appendix~\ref{app:significance}).

do: the correlation between halves, corrected to full length (Spearman--Brown),
gives a reliability of 0.78 for WER$_B$ and 0.75 for relative gain. This is what
justifies ranking speakers by their gain when selecting them for

\paragraph{Decision rule.}
Before the longer runs we fixed a rule on the full test, comparing each

Personalization would count as growing with data if the difference were at least
+2 points with its interval above zero for at least two of the four speakers,
and as not growing if every interval contained zero with an upper end below +2.
Neither holds: three intervals meet the second condition, but 23558's lies
entirely below zero ($-5.6$, $[-7.7,-3.6]$). The rule is therefore undecided by
its wording, while the result points against growth.

\section{Significance Testing}
\label{app:significance}

Every comparison in Section~\ref{sec:adaptation} is paired: two models transcribe the
same test segments of one speaker, so we ask whether one makes fewer errors than the
other on those segments.

\paragraph{What is compared.}
For the gain of an adapter over arm~B, the difference is
$\text{WER}_B-\text{WER}_\text{adapted}$. For personalization, it is
$\text{WER}_\text{control}-\text{WER}_\text{own}$: the own adapter and the control on
the same segments. Both are pooled differences, the sum of each model's word errors
over the segments divided by the sum of reference words.

\paragraph{Resampling.}
We draw as many segments as the speaker's test set holds, with replacement, and
recompute the pooled difference on the draw. This is repeated 2{,}000 times. The
same draw is applied to both models, so a hard segment counts for both or for
neither, and the variation between segments largely cancels. We resample whole
segments rather than words because errors within a segment are not independent:
a misheard name or a runaway transcription produces several at once.

\paragraph{Interval and p-value.}
The 95\% interval is the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile of the 2{,}000 differences,
in absolute WER; dividing by WER$_B$ gives it in the relative units of
Section~\ref{sec:adaptation}. The two-sided p-value is twice the smaller of the
shares of draws at or below zero and at or above zero, so it resolves p-values
down to 0.001, and smaller ones are reported as $p<0.001$.

\paragraph{Multiple comparisons.}
Each speaker--seed pair is tested on its own, without correction. If own
adapters and controls did not differ, about two of 36 pairs would fall below
$p=0.05$ by chance, and about one of them with a positive sign. We observe 8
(clean test) and 16 (full test) significantly positive pairs and none
significantly negative. Per-speaker claims rest on agreement across all three
seeds rather than on a single p-value.

\section{Bootstrap for the Speaker-Level Error Map}
\label{app:boot-errormap}

The per-speaker intervals of Section~\ref{sec:errormap}, and the bars in
Figure~\ref{fig:gain}, come from a simpler bootstrap than
Appendix~\ref{app:significance}'s, run once per speaker on arm~A's and arm~B's
transcriptions of that speaker's one-hour sample.

\paragraph{Resampling.}
The speaker's segments are drawn with replacement, as many as the sample holds, 1{,}000
times. The same draw is used for both arms. Each draw gives a pooled WER$_A$ and
WER$_B$ (the sum of word errors over the drawn segments divided by the sum of reference
words) and their difference, the absolute gain $\text{WER}_A-\text{WER}_B$.

\paragraph{Intervals.}
The 95\% interval of WER$_A$, WER$_B$ and the gain is the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile of
the 1{,}000 values. No p-value is computed: a speaker's improvement under arm~B is
called beyond sampling noise when the interval of their gain lies entirely above zero,
as it does for all 261 speakers with at least 20 segments.

\paragraph{Difference from Appendix~\ref{app:significance}.}
The resampling is the same (whole segments, paired across the two models), but it uses
1{,}000 draws instead of 2{,}000, compares the two base models instead of adapters, and
reports intervals only.
