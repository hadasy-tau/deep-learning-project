01What changed since the proposal

Three findings reshape Stage 2 before a single GPU hour is spent. Each one invalidates part of the original plan, and together they point at a sharper question.

Stage 1 result

The population fine-tune already helps everyone

It removes ~50% of relative error for essentially every speaker, hurts nobody, and wer_A/wer_B correlate at ≈ +0.8. The worst-served speakers got there by starting hard, not by gaining less. So “fine-tune the poorly-served” is no longer the open question.

Contamination

The target model already saw this corpus

ivrit-ai/whisper-large-v3 was trained on ~4,700 h of Knesset plenums — the same source as VoxKnesset. Any gain measured on it sits on top of heavy prior exposure to these exact speakers.

Data shape

The corpus cannot feed Whisper as-is

VoxKnesset enforces a minimum of 30 s per segment; the mean is ~124 s and Stage 1 hit a 4.2-hour one. Whisper’s window is exactly 30 s. And the official 80/20 split is speaker-disjoint — precisely backwards for personalization.

The question, restated

Once a population-level Hebrew fine-tune has already taken half the error, is there anything speaker-specific left — and if so, how many minutes of one person’s voice does it take to get it?

Either answer is a contribution. If personalization on top of ivrit-ai buys little, that is a real and useful negative result — and the uncontaminated model arm still yields the full recipe curve.

Where this sits against prior adaptation work

Classical ASR adaptation spent years trying to make representations speaker-invariant — i-vector and x-vector conditioning, adversarial speaker-invariant training — because a recognizer that ignores who is talking generalizes better across a population. This project does the exact inverse: it deliberately specializes a model to one person and treats the loss of invariance as the product rather than the failure. That inversion is worth stating plainly in the related-work section; it frames the encoder axis below, where the same question appears as how much speaker-specific information is the encoder still throwing away?

02Choosing the panel

No GPU and no Stage-2 training code. It belongs in the Stage-1 notebook, alongside the split-half correction already in §9, and it must be settled before any of the design below matters.

Selection must not select on noise

Every speaker’s measured WER is true WER + sampling noise from a finite set of segments. Taking the worst decile by measured WER selects a mixture of genuinely-hard speakers and speakers who merely drew a bad sample. Re-measure them and the bad-luck component does not repeat, so their WER falls — a gain that looks caused by the fine-tune and would have appeared with no fine-tune at all.

The principle is the one Stage 1 already uses: the variable you select on and the variable you measure the outcome on must not share noise.

Rather than a separate split-half, get this from the session split that D2 defines anyway. Define the session-disjoint split first, rank speakers using only their train-side sessions, and leave personal-test untouched until final scoring. Noise independence then falls out of ordinary don’t-touch-the-test-set hygiene, and there is one split in the project instead of two.

Ordering cost of doing it this way

Splitting by session requires temporal metadata for every candidate speaker, not just the four you end up picking. The session id is carried in the filename (speaker_session_start_end.wav), so that metadata is free — but the index stage still has to run before selection, which means this section is not quite purely Stage-1-local.

Entry requirements

≥ 3 h of speech and ≥ 8 distinct sessions, so an 80-minute training budget plus disjoint dev and test is possible. Stage 1’s ≥ 20-segment reliability floor applies on top.

A third requirement follows from the power calculation: enough speech left over for a personal-test sized to that speaker’s WER, rather than a flat number of minutes for everyone. Resolution tracks WER — a low-WER speaker has few errors left to remove, so detecting the same relative change takes far more test data. This binds hardest at the low-WER end, so S4 must be chosen for volume.

03Design decisions

D1Two model arms, not one

Stage 1 already produced per-speaker baselines for both models, so the second arm costs no new baseline work — it extends the existing gain_rel table with a third column.

Model arms
ArmModelRole
A openai/whisper-large-v3 Positive control. Hebrew is in its multilingual mix but low-resource, and it has no Knesset-specific training. Stage 1’s ~50% median relative gain puts it at roughly twice arm B’s WER — a working model with ample headroom.
B ivrit-ai/whisper-large-v3 The real target. Gains here are on top of ~4,700 h of Knesset exposure.

Arm A is not an upper bound — it is the control that makes a null result on B interpretable. Given Stage 1 suggests B’s residual may be small, you need one arm where you are confident an effect exists:

if the recipe shows nothing on B, arm A says whether that is
because nothing is left, or because the pipeline is broken

Two things follow. First, arm A’s gains are mostly Hebrew and Knesset-style learning, not personalization — its Δ domain will be large, so only Δ personal − Δ domain is comparable across arms. Second, arm A does not get the full grid: reference config plus the data-budget axis is enough for it to do its job, which roughly halves its cost.

The A-vs-B gap after personalization remains the headline number, and it is what turns the thesis question into something measurable.

D2Session-disjoint splits, built by us

Partition each speaker’s sessions instead, using the session id carried in the filename — speaker_session_start_end.wav. Sessions are ordered by age, which is constant within any one (speaker, session) and so gives a clean chronology. D3The cross-speaker control is mandatory

If the two are equal, the gain is domain adaptation and there is no personalization at all.

Personalization and shareability are the same axis, pointing opposite ways

The transfer cell measures one quantity with two consequences, not two separate outcomes. Write r = Δ(others) / Δ(S):

  • r → 1 — the adapter is generic. Ideal for deployment: train once on one person, ship to all 393. And precisely because of that, no personalization has occurred.
  • r → 0 — genuinely personal. The scientifically interesting result, and the expensive one to deploy: one adapter per speaker.
  • in between — both components present, and each can be sized.

The two goals are therefore in real tension, and mapping that tension is part of what this project delivers. Report both gains, not the ratio alone — r can look large simply because Δ(S) is small. The personalization quantity is the difference, Δ(S) − Δ(others).

The site axis carries a prediction that differs between arms

Arm B has already absorbed 4,700 h of Knesset: protocol style and parliamentary vocabulary are learned at population level, so the residual should be acoustic — encoder-side. Arm A has learned neither Hebrew formatting nor Knesset vocabulary, so its gain should sit decoder-side. State this before running it. A prediction that flips between arms is falsifiable in a way “we expect the encoder matters” is not, and if it holds it is the most quotable result in the study.

Insensitivity is itself the finding

The optimization and capacity sweeps already give a spread across configurations — no separate search is needed to read it. If the best configuration barely beats the reference, report that: it means the recipe transfers without per-speaker tuning, which is exactly what makes it a recipe rather than four tuned models. Report the range across the sweep alongside the winner, not the winner alone.

Start the sharing axis at one speaker, and make it configurable

Run the three sharing configurations for one speaker and one subgroup definition first, rather than the whole panel. Both the target speaker and subgroup membership are config parameters, never hardcoded, so the same code answers two different questions: hold the speaker fixed and vary the subgroup definition to ask what kind of similarity matters; or hold the definition fixed and vary the speaker to ask whether the answer generalizes. Expand only once one cell has told you which question is worth the compute.

The released metadata supports genuinely demographic subgroups rather than improvised ones. Three definition families worth contrasting: demographic (speaker_place_of_birth and speaker_year_of_aliya are the closest proxies for accent, alongside religion, nationality, religious orientation, age and gender), behavioural (Stage-1 error profile, or per-speaker wpm), and acoustic (x-vector/ECAPA distance). If demographic similarity predicts transfer as well as acoustic similarity does, that is a useful result in itself — subgroup assignment would need no audio.

D5Report the cost, not just the gain

Every personalized model is also scored on other Knesset speakers and on an out-of-domain Hebrew set (FLEURS he_il, or an ivrit-ai crowd-transcribe split). A recipe must state what it breaks. Track hallucination and repetition rate too — fine-tuned Whisper is known to regress there, and Stage 1 explicitly flagged looping decoders as an unhandled case.

Two ways to buy generality back — as a frontier, not an optimum

Both trade personal-test WER for everything else, so the headline recipe runs unconstrained. Neither is a training aid; they are deployment knobs.

  • α-scaling — scale the LoRA adapter down at inference. Traces the whole trade-off curve post hoc from a single unconstrained run. Free.
  • KL-to-base — add λ · KL(p_base ‖ p_tuned) on generic Hebrew audio to the loss, so the model is penalized for drifting where you don’t want it to. Costs ~2–2.5× step time, since every speaker batch needs a generic batch beside it. With LoRA the teacher is free: with model.disable_adapter(): gives base predictions without a second 1.5 B model in VRAM.

Sweep λ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 5} and plot personal WER against other-speaker WER as a Pareto frontier. Do not search for an optimal λ — naming one asserts an exchange rate between your speaker’s errors and everyone else’s, which nothing in the study justifies. Test α-scaling first: if its free frontier matches KL’s, drop KL entirely.

D6Regularization

Dropout, weight decay, early stopping on personal-dev, checkpoint averaging (the ivrit-ai model is itself a three-checkpoint weight average). These do improve personal-test WER, by fighting overfitting — and they apply at every budget, because 80 minutes against a 1.5 B model is still a heavily over-parameterized regime. The budget-dependent question is not whether to use them but how their optimal strength scales with minutes, which is itself part of the recipe.

One arm deserves explicit ablation rather than assumption:

  • Augmentation — SpecAugment, speed perturbation 0.9/1.1, noise and gain. Note the tension: augmentation deliberately smears the speaker’s acoustics, which may be counterproductive for personalization specifically.

D7Evaluation protocol: short-form primary, long-form secondary

Stage 1 scored whole recordings — long-form, up to 4.2 hours. Stage 2 trains on ≤ 30 s chunks and would naturally score per chunk — short-form. These are not the same measurement: long-form decoding adds drift, context carryover and hallucination that short-form does not have. Reporting one and comparing against the other silently mixes two effects.

  • Primary — short-form, per chunk. Matches the training distribution, yields far more test items for the paired bootstrap, and removes long-form decoding pathologies as a confounder of the personalization effect.
  • Secondary — long-form, on personal-test recordings. Deployment realism, and the only figure comparable to Stage 1.

Report both. The gap between them is where D5’s hallucination and repetition metric actually shows up — a personalized model that improves short-form while degrading long-form has learned something that does not survive real use, and only running both will reveal it.

04Pipeline

The real work runs on a GPU VM; Colab is only a proof-of-concept target for checking the pipeline executes end to end. That removes session survival as an architecture driver, and with it two constraints that shaped the earlier lora-lens design:

  • No streaming. Materialize the panel speakers and control pool to local disk once — budget ~20–40 GB out of VoxKnesset’s 275 GB — and stop reasoning about HTTP range reads after the index stage.
  • Resumability is a convenience, not the design. Keep the staged CLI and per-stage artifacts under output_dir because they make partial re-runs cheap, but a plain config-driven package is enough. The cells.csv manifest stays only as a way to drive several GPUs in parallel.

The dedicated VM also puts full fine-tuning genuinely on the table, depending on the card — which is what keeps axis 1 from collapsing into a comparison of LoRA variants.

The chunking stage — the main new engineering

Segments average ~124 s against Whisper’s 30 s window and carry no timestamps, so text must be aligned to audio before anything can be trained. Two routes, cheapest first.

Primary — text-anchored chunking, no GPU and no new model

Your Stage-1 dumps already contain a Whisper-produced segmentation of every recording (segments_json, with start/end per hypothesis segment). Reuse it:

  1. Take the hypothesis segments and their [start, end] for a recording.
  2. Normalize hypothesis and protocol reference with normalize_he().
  3. Word-level alignment between the two — rapidfuzz’s Levenshtein.opcodes, already in the stack.
  4. Map each hypothesis segment boundary to a reference word index; cut the reference there.
  5. Emit (audio[start:end], reference_slice) pairs, packing consecutive spans up to 30 s.

Boundaries only need to be approximately right — cuts fall roughly every 20 s — so local ASR errors do not propagate. Only the ct2 dump carries segments_json, so boundaries are B-derived: the same boundary-only bias the caveat below already accepts. Note that some hypothesis segments run past 30 s, so cuts are made inside them by interpolating word times rather than trusting segment boundaries alone.

Fallback — audio forced alignment

If the text-anchored route aligns poorly, use stable-ts with ivrit-ai/whisper-large-v3: the same tooling and model family VoxKnesset’s own authors used, so boundaries match the corpus’s construction. Greedily pack word spans into ≤ 30 s chunks, breaking at the longest intra-window pause.

Either route: compute a per-chunk alignment score, keep it as a column, and do not filter on it. Nothing usable is released, so this is our score, not VoxKnesset’s. Carry Stage 1’s wpm filter (30–350 reference words/minute) forward — it is what caught the 4.2-hour/126-word segment, and chunking does not make that class of mismatch disappear.

Circularity caveat — why we do not filter on alignment quality

Both routes derive boundaries from a Whisper model, so alignment is most accurate exactly where that model already understands the audio. Where it struggles — accent, crosstalk, noise — boundaries drift and our alignment score drops. Filter on that score and you have quietly deleted B’s hard cases: B’s baseline reads optimistically low, the headroom personalization would fill is removed, and worst, arms A and B end up compared on a test set curated by B — which biases the headline of the entire design.

Decision: keep every chunk. The bias is then confined to boundary placement rather than set membership, and no segment is removed for being hard. Note the reasoning briefly in the notebook where the score is computed.

The two filters differ in kind, which is why one survives and one does not: wpm is model-independent — it catches text that does not correspond to its audio at all. Alignment quality is model-dependent — it catches audio the aligner found hard, which is the very thing under study. If a reviewer pushes, the airtight fix is to align with a CTC model outside the Whisper family (ctc-forced-aligner / MMS) so alignment errors are uncorrelated with either arm.

Reuse from Stage 1, verbatim

  • normalize_he() — the niqqud and geresh/gershayim normalizer, including the geresh→space rule. Freeze it; use the identical function for every arm. Do not re-derive it.
  • rapidfuzz Levenshtein.distance over word lists and characters, storing error counts, not rates, so speaker WER stays total_errors / total_words.
  • The wpm sanity filter and the ≥ 20-segment reliability floor.
  • The split-half noise correction from §9 of stage1_basic.ipynb.
  • The existing HF inference dumps as the baseline column — no re-transcription needed for Stage 1 numbers.

Whisper fine-tuning gotchas to encode in the training stage

  • Set language="he", task="transcribe". forced_decoder_ids no longer exists as of transformers 5 — GenerationConfig dropped it, so set language and task on the generation config and leave suppress_tokens = [].
  • The tokenizer prepends <|startoftranscript|> and shift_tokens_right prepends decoder_start_token_id — the same id. Strip one, or the decoder trains on a doubled start token.
  • Mask pad labels with -100; keep the special-token prefix identical between train and eval.
  • Gradient checkpointing needs use_cache = False plus enable_input_require_grads() under PEFT.
  • target_modules accepts a regex string in PEFT — use it to isolate encoder (.*model\.encoder.*\.(q_proj|v_proj)), decoder self-attn (.*self_attn.*) and decoder cross-attn (.*encoder_attn.*). Without that separation the site axis cannot be run at all.
  • Decide once whether to train with timestamp tokens and hold it constant; ivrit-ai’s training data used them, so dropping them may degrade timestamp behaviour.
  • Fast eval path: merge the adapter, then ct2-transformers-converter — it drops straight into the faster-whisper harness Stage 1 already used.

07Verification

  • Chunking — listen to 20 random chunks and confirm the audio matches its text. Assert every chunk is ≤ 30 s and that chunk text concatenates back to the original protocol text.
  • Split integrity — zero session overlap between personal-train/dev/test; nested budgets are strict subsets; the control pool shares no speaker with the panel.
  • Baseline reproduction — compare like with like. Stage 1 measured long-form WER over whole recordings, so the gate must re-score personal-test in long-form with the base ivrit-ai model and reproduce that speaker’s Stage-1 wer_B within noise. Comparing Stage-1 long-form against Stage-2 chunk-level WER would fail for reasons that have nothing to do with a bug. If the like-for-like check fails, chunking or normalization changed the metric and every later comparison is invalid — hard abort, the way lora-lens gates on _check_base_final_layer().
  • Short-vs-long sanity — also record the base model’s short-form WER on the same chunks. The long-form/short-form gap for the base model is the reference against which every tuned model’s gap is read (D7).
  • Training sanity — overfit a 20-example subset to near-zero loss before any real run.
  • Statistics — paired bootstrap over segments (base vs. tuned on the same segments), plus the insertion/deletion/substitution breakdown. If gains come mostly from fewer insertions, the model learned protocol style rather than the speaker.
  • End-to-end smoke — one speaker × one budget × one method on turbo, all stages, before committing to the grid.

08Open risks

  1. Contamination. The ivrit-ai model likely saw these speakers and possibly these exact utterances. Arm A exists specifically so the recipe is measurable on an uncontaminated model. Report exposure explicitly rather than claiming a clean baseline.
  2. Non-verbatim references. Protocol text is not what was said. There is a WER floor no amount of personalization can cross, and part of any gain is style learning — which is what the cross-speaker control and the I/D/S breakdown exist to detect.
  3. The null result. Stage 1’s uniform-gain finding makes a small arm-B effect plausible. The design survives that: arm A still yields the full recipe, and “personalization adds little once a strong population model exists” is defensible — provided stage0_gate establishes that the study could have detected an effect.
